# The Entire History of Tata, and the Diamond That Saved It: creative treatment

*Built on Bharat · Film 1 · Series: Empires of India · Creative director's pitch (PLAYBOOK §2.1)*
*Owner's choices (2026-10-24): the full history with the diamond as its spine; end on the open 2026 question.*
*Facts come from the owner's fact-checked dossier (`docs/research/owner-tata-2026-10-24/`) and our checks
(`docs/research/topic-choice-2026-10-24/report.md`). Every line is verified again in `claims.csv` before recording,
and the 2026 dispute is re-checked at script lock and again before upload.*

## Logline
In 1924, with India's first steel plant about to go cold, Sir Dorabji Tata pledged his wife's diamond to a bank to pay
the workers. Eight years later he left everything, the diamond included, to a charity. That charity still owns more
of Tata Sons than any family member ever has, and in 2026 it is at the centre of the fight over who runs India's
biggest business group.

## Why this film, now
- **Demand:** "tata group history", "tata history in english", "tata empire", "who owns tata now", "tata trusts vs
  tata sons" (YouTube autocomplete, India, 2026-10-24).
- **Supply:** the big films are Hindi (Dhruv Rathee 18M, 4 years old) or old English (Business Casual 2.2M, 7 years;
  ColdFusion 5.3M, 10 years). None tells the 2026 ownership fight, and none is built around one human thread.
- **News window:** the Trusts–Sons dispute and the RBI listing decision are live (September 2026).

## The feeling
- **Start:** a held breath. A velvet case on a bank counter; a jungle town of families waiting for their wages.
- **Middle:** pride with open eyes. Indians built what they were told they couldn't, and also made expensive mistakes.
- **End:** a quiet, unresolved question that belongs to every Indian: who should own a company built "for the love of
  India"?

## The spine
One object carries 158 years: **the Jubilee Diamond.**
1. **Planted** in the cold open (1924): pledged to save Tata Steel.
2. **Moved** at the midpoint (1931–32): Lady Meherbai dies of leukaemia, Sir Dorabji dies a year later, and his whole
   fortune, the diamond and his Tata Sons shares included, goes to a new trust. Its money later helps build the Tata
   Memorial cancer hospital. *(Sale year conflicts: 1935 or 1937; the film says "a few years later".)*
3. **Paid off** at the end: that trust still holds about 28% of Tata Sons, the largest single stake. With the other
   Tata Trusts it holds about two thirds, which is why no Tata tops a rich list, and why the 2026 fight is about the
   trusts.

## Signature moments
1. **The match cut.** The diamond's facets catch furnace light on the bank counter → hard cut on the same orange to
   molten steel in 1912.
2. **The ledger of 8,000.** A share register scrolls faster and faster as the counter climbs to ₹2.32 crore. Real if
   the archive allows; a labelled reconstruction if not.
3. **The furnace cutaway.** A hero 3D section of a 1910s blast furnace, built from period drawings (ILLUSTRATION).
4. **The ownership machine.** Tata Sons drawn as a steel structure; the trusts' share fills it like molten metal
   pouring into a mould, and the diamond sits at the base.
5. **Callback across a century.** Upcott's boast typed on a 1907 memo → the 2007 Corus headline sliding over it.

## Tone guardrails (PLAYBOOK §4.1)
- Proud, never hagiography: the opium-era trade, the 1922 strike, Corus's cost, the Nano's losses and the Air India
  losses are all in the film.
- Legends are called legends (the Taj insult, the Upcott line, the Ford quotes).
- AI171: one respectful passage, no speculation about cause, no crash imagery, no victims' faces.
- The 2026 dispute: both sides' positions, attributed; no villain; re-checked before upload.

## The visual world
Charcoal and bronze, with one colour per era: **furnace orange** (1868–1924), **sepia and aviation blue** (JRD),
**steel grey and boardroom glass** (Ratan), **cleanroom cyan** (now). The diamond is the only white-hot point of
light in the whole film. Archive photos (public-domain pre-1950 portraits, Commons, Tata heritage with permission),
period newspapers, share certificates on an isometric desk, maps of Sakchi and of the Karachi–Bombay route.

## Sound palette
Furnace roar, metal, steam whistles, a Puss Moth engine, a typewriter for the memos. Score: low strings for the
trader, tabla pulse for steel, solo cello for the trust, brass warmth for JRD, a tense pulse for the boardrooms, and
silence before the ownership reveal.
